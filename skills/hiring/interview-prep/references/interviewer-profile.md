# Interviewer profile — v1.1

These are the accepted preferences behind this skill, not assumptions about
every interviewer or hiring round. Current instructions take precedence.

## Style and priorities

The interviewer prefers a small number of practical questions that feel curious,
direct, conversational, and slightly probing. Questions should be usable aloud
without rewriting. Avoid formal HR language, generic icebreakers, exam-style
trivia, and a scripted STAR checklist.

Typical interests are how the candidate thinks, makes decisions, handles
ambiguity, communicates, owns work, manages stakeholders, and connects technical
work to business impact. These are a menu of possible interests, not required
coverage. Let the candidate and the interviewer's current focus choose the mix.

## Intake and quantity

The usual entry point is an attached PDF plus “prepare interview questions.”
Read the CV first, then ask only missing context that materially changes the
result. Offer candidate-specific numbered options so the interviewer can answer
`#2`, `#4`, or a combination. Do not equate a short opening prompt with permission
to skip material intake, or re-ask context already established in this round.

Depth matters more than quantity. Two main questions often fit a 15-minute
session; three can fit 20–30 minutes. A 15–20 minute interview may use either,
depending on the selected priorities. Longer sessions can start with 3–4.
These are defaults, not a reason to ignore an explicit requested count.

## Ownership and judgment

Distinguish the team's outcome from this person's responsibility, decisions,
contribution, and influence. Ask which decision was theirs when the answer is
mostly “we,” but do not repeat an ownership probe after it has been answered.

Prefer “What made you choose that approach?” over “What methodology did you
use?” Explore alternatives, constraints, changes of mind, and observable results.
Treat impressive claims as invitations to understand, not accusations.

## Technical and stakeholder depth

Technical interviews here often aim to understand whether the person understands
their own work and can explain design decisions and trade-offs. Do not assume
the interviewer wants a coding examination. When relevant, ask the candidate to
explain a real decision to a business stakeholder; do not use that same device
in every interview.

Anchor stakeholder questions to actual teams, dependencies, or delivery settings
in the CV. Leadership can mean influence or initiative ownership without direct
reports. For people managers, explore decisions made through other people when
that is relevant to the hiring role.

## Language and flow

Follow the requested or established interview language. Natural Bahasa Indonesia
can comfortably retain English technical/business terms:

> “Apa yang kamu ubah supaya project-nya bisa balik on track?”

> “Waktu itu, gimana kamu menjelaskan trade-off-nya ke stakeholder bisnis?”

These illustrate voice, not reusable questions. Actual questions still need the
current candidate's evidence. Match the user's level of formality while keeping
candidate-facing wording respectful and neutral.

Open with substance. When a career move or motivation question is useful, put
it toward the end unless motivation is explicitly the main screen. Explore the
candidate's expectations without assuming why they applied or judging a title
change. Motivation is optional if it does not fit the chosen focus or time.

## Continuity

Within the same hiring round, reuse the established role, duration, language,
and interviewer preferences when still applicable. Rebuild the candidate
fingerprint for every new CV. The skill is not permanently limited to TPM,
a particular employer, senior candidates, or the two development dry runs.
