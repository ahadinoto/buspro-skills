# Interview question quality bar — v1.1

A main question earns its place because of this candidate, this role, and what
the interviewer wants to learn. Labels and company names alone are insufficient.

## The five anti-template gates

1. **Candidate:** Would the same question work unchanged for most candidates?
   Remove names and company labels mentally. If the substance is still generic,
   find the distinctive decision, transition, claim, or uncertainty to probe.
2. **CV:** Is the anchor actually supported by the CV or explicitly supplied
   professional context? Keep metrics, responsibilities, and technologies exact.
   Do not convert a plausible inference into a factual premise. A sparse CV may
   justify a modest exploratory question; disclose the evidence limit.
3. **Role:** Will the answer help assess an actual responsibility of the hiring
   role? A fascinating story unrelated to the role is a weak use of short time.
4. **Interviewer:** Does it address the selected priority, at a depth this
   interviewer can use? Honor numbered selections and avoid re-covering areas
   assigned to another interviewer unless requested.
5. **Conversation:** Is it concise and natural to say aloud in the chosen
   language? Does it ask one main thing, with room for a responsive follow-up?

Revise a failed gate before delivery. Do not show a checklist for every question
unless requested. If evidence is missing, acknowledge it instead of pretending
all gates passed.

## Personalization that changes the question

The examples below are fictional teaching examples, not candidate facts.

| Supplied evidence | Weak question | More useful question |
|---|---|---|
| Moved 12 services while the platform stayed live | “At Company X, tell me about a challenging project.” | “During the move of those 12 live services, what was the hardest decision about what to migrate first?” |
| Reduced processing time by 60% | “How do you improve processes?” | “What was creating most of the delay before that 60% reduction, and which change made the difference?” |
| Co-led a multi-team migration; personal scope unclear | “Tell me about the migration.” | “Which part of that migration was yours to decide, and where did another lead have the final call?” |
| Changed from engineering to program work | “Why did you change careers?” | “After moving from building the solution to coordinating its delivery, what changed most in how you made decisions?” |

An optional probe should target the remaining uncertainty, such as how the
reduction was measured or which decision came from the candidate. Do not bundle
every possible probe into the main question.

## Common failures to remove

1. **Decorative specificity:** A company or project name followed by an otherwise
   generic “difficult stakeholder” question.
2. **Invented tension:** Assuming there was conflict, risk, failure, or a deadline
   when the source does not say so. Ask whether it happened or choose another hook.
3. **Coverage by template:** Forcing one behavioral, one technical, and one
   management question regardless of the actual CV and selected priorities.
4. **Ownership by accusation:** Treating a large metric or junior title as proof
   of overclaiming. Probe responsibility and decision authority neutrally.
5. **Checklist sentences:** Asking for context, role, actions, all stakeholders,
   results, and lessons in one breath. Keep one main uncertainty per question.
6. **Repetitive angles:** Three differently named projects all used to ask the
   same question. Vary what is learned, not merely the opening words.
7. **Mechanical communication tests:** Asking every candidate to simplify a
   technical topic whether or not it is useful for this role and interviewer.
8. **Poor sequence:** Opening with motivation by habit, or adding motivation
   after the user selected only two other priorities for a short session.
9. **Language mismatch:** Translating common technical terms awkwardly or using
   formal Indonesian that the interviewer would have to rewrite.
10. **Cross-candidate leakage:** Reusing a prior candidate's facts, concerns, or
    question structure without evidence in the new CV.

## What counts as evidence in an answer

Listen for personal responsibility, reasoning, alternatives, constraints,
observable outcomes, and the ability to explain decisions. A polished story is
not proof of strong performance. A vague story is a reason for a precise probe,
not a basis to declare dishonesty. Keep any assessment tied to job-relevant
evidence, and never infer sensitive personal traits from the CV.
